In February, PepsiCo cut the price of its most famous snacks by up to 15%. Eight months later, its North American snack business sells 0.5% more. And its revenue is exactly flat on a year ago.

That is the whole story in one line. A price cut is easy to sign and very hard to end.

On 8 October 2026, PepsiCo reported its third quarter. Total revenue grew 5.6% to $25.3 billion. Wall Street called it a beat. But in North America, its biggest market, organic revenue did not move at all. And the company cut its profit growth forecast for the full year.

Most people will read this as a story about a tired shopper. It is not. It is a story about what happens after you say yes to the discount. Because the real question is not *did the price cut work?* The real question is: **can PepsiCo ever take it back?**

## What happened: two strategies, one result

For years, PepsiCo kept raising prices. In the last quarter of 2025, its global prices rose 4.5%, and North American drink prices rose 7% (<a href="https://jamaica-gleaner.com/article/news/20260203/pepsico-plans-price-cuts-demand-its-drinks-and-snacks-slip" target="_blank" rel="noopener">AP via The Gleaner</a>). Shoppers pushed back. Snack volumes fell, and drink volumes in North America fell 4%.

So on 3 February 2026, PepsiCo changed direction. It announced cuts of up to almost 15% on Lay's, Doritos, Cheetos and Tostitos, just before the Super Bowl (<a href="https://www.npr.org/2026/02/03/nx-s1-5697941/pepsi-prices-cheetos-doritos-super-bowl-snacks" target="_blank" rel="noopener">NPR</a>). The move was part of a deal with activist investor Elliott, which also cut the product range by a fifth. CEO Ramon Laguarta called affordability the biggest thing stopping low- and middle-income shoppers from buying more.

Here is the interesting part. PepsiCo cut prices on snacks, but it kept raising prices on drinks. That makes Q3 a live experiment inside one company (<a href="https://investor.pepsico.com/docs/pepsico-5v9wci20/media/Files/investors/q3-2026-earnings-release.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener">PepsiCo Q3 2026 release</a>):

| North America, Q3 2026 | Snacks (prices cut) | Drinks (prices kept up) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Net price change | −1% | +3% |
| Volume change | +0.5% | −3% |
| Organic revenue change | 0% | 0% |

One team cut price and won a few more bags. The other team held price and lost buyers. **Both landed on zero.**

The company then cut its 2026 forecast for core profit growth (in constant currency) to 1–2%. Before, it had pointed to the low end of 4–6%. Total revenue still grew 5.6%, thanks to acquisitions and markets outside the US, where organic revenue rose 8%.

## What everyone is getting wrong

The common take goes like this: the consumer is squeezed, PepsiCo listened, and price cuts need time to work. It sounds kind. It is also wrong.

**Price was the reason shoppers gave. It was not the reason they left.** When people stop buying your product, they rarely say "your value is weak." They say "it's too expensive." Those two sentences sound the same. They are not the same problem.

Look at where shoppers went. They did not just buy fewer snacks. They moved to the store's own brand. US store brands sold a record $282.8 billion in 2025, and their sales grew 3.3% while big brands grew 1.2% (<a href="https://plma.com/article/2026-private-label-report-2828-billion-sales" target="_blank" rel="noopener">PLMA</a>). In the first half of 2026, store brands reached a record 23.8% of all units sold. Their unit sales rose while big brands fell (<a href="https://massmarketretailers.com/plma-store-brands-unit-sales-outperform-in-first-half-of-2026/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Circana data via PLMA</a>).

Those shoppers found out something important: the cheaper bag is fine. Once a customer learns that, a 15% cut does not bring them back. The gap is still there, and now the brand has less money to close it.

There is a second mistake. People think a price cut is a short campaign. It is not. A campaign has an end date. A new price becomes the new normal in the customer's head. Pull it back, and you do not look like a brand returning to normal. You look like a brand raising prices again.

## Rule 26: A discount can become your longest contract

Every discount is a contract. You sign it in one meeting, and you pay it for years.

The contract is not written on paper. It is written in the customer's memory. The moment you cut the price, the customer learns three things:

1. The old price was not the real price.
2. If they wait, or complain, the price comes down.
3. The new price is the fair price, and anything above it is greed.

No marketing budget erases those lessons fast. That is why the contract is so long.

### The math nobody shows the board

A price cut needs a lot of extra volume just to stand still. Here is the simple formula for how much more you must sell to keep the same gross profit:

> **Extra volume needed = discount ÷ (gross margin − discount)**

Take a product with a 40% gross margin. This is an example, not PepsiCo's real margin:

| Price cut | Extra volume needed just to make the same profit |
| --- | --- |
| 5% | +14% |
| 10% | +33% |
| 15% | +60% |
| 20% | +100% |

A 15% cut needs 60% more sales just to break even. PepsiCo's snacks got 0.5%. That is not a failure of execution. It is what usually happens. Volume almost never jumps as much as the spreadsheet hopes.

The discount did one thing well. It stopped the bleeding. But stopping the bleeding at a lower price means you now live with a smaller business that does the same amount of work.

## The next 12 months: who wins and who loses

PepsiCo is not alone. General Mills also said this year that it would discount about two-thirds of its range (<a href="https://www.npr.org/2026/02/03/nx-s1-5697941/pepsi-prices-cheetos-doritos-super-bowl-snacks" target="_blank" rel="noopener">NPR</a>). Many big brands are now at the same crossroads. Here is where I think it goes.

**Store brands win first.** Supermarkets are the quiet winners of every brand price war. When a big brand cuts its price, the store brand looks fine next to it. When the brand raises its price again, the store brand looks smart. Store brands win both ways. Expect retailers to push more premium own-label ranges, better packs and loyalty-only prices.

**Brands that add value win next.** The brands that come out stronger will not be the cheapest. They will be the ones that give the customer a new reason to pay: a better product, a new flavour, a health benefit, a smaller premium pack, a moment people care about. PepsiCo itself is now talking about new flavours, more zero-sugar options and more marketing for its drinks. That is the right instinct (<a href="https://www.benzinga.com/markets/earnings/26/10/62244383/pepsico-admits-its-soda-business-is-falling-behind-rivals" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Benzinga</a>). It is also slower and harder than a price cut.

**Brands that keep cutting lose.** Each new cut buys less volume than the one before. And each one makes the next price rise harder to sell.

**Every business will feel this pull, not just snacks.** Hotels, agencies, software, B2B services: the same temptation shows up in every bad quarter. A big client asks for 15% off "just this year." A sales team misses target and asks for a promotion. The board wants volume now. The discount always looks cheaper than the slow work of building value. It almost never is.

## What a good sales leader does instead

The job of a sales leader is not to defend the price. It is to make the price feel small next to the value. Here is how to do that when the pressure to discount arrives.

1. **Ask what the customer is really saying.** "Too expensive" usually means "I don't see enough value" or "I found something good enough." Find out which one before you touch the price.
2. **Trade, never give.** If a price must move, get something back: a longer term, a bigger volume, faster payment, a case study, a referral. A discount with nothing in return teaches the customer that asking works.
3. **Put an end date on paper.** If you must run a promotion, make the end date part of the deal. A price with no end date becomes the new price.
4. **Change the offer, not the price.** A smaller pack, a lighter package, a different service level. Give the customer a cheaper option without cheapening the main one.
5. **Run the math before the meeting.** Know how much extra volume a cut needs to break even. Put that number in front of the board. Most discounts die quietly when that number is on the table.
6. **Add value people can feel.** Speed, service, a better experience, proof of results. Today AI makes it cheap to research every client and personalise every proposal. Use it to add value, not to send more discounts faster.

A discount is the fastest way to get a yes. Value is the slowest. Next year, value is still paying you. With a discount, you are still paying for it.

## The last word

PepsiCo will be fine. It has close to $100 billion in yearly revenue and strong markets outside the US. Most businesses do not have that cushion.

So before you sign the next discount, ask one simple question: *am I solving this quarter, or signing a contract with next year?*

That is Rule 26 of 55. A discount can become your longest contract.

## FAQ

### Why did PepsiCo cut prices in 2026?

After years of price rises, shoppers bought less and switched to cheaper store brands. In February 2026, PepsiCo cut prices on Lay's, Doritos, Cheetos and Tostitos by up to almost 15% to win them back.

### Did PepsiCo's price cuts work?

Only partly. In Q3 2026, North American snack volume rose 0.5%, but prices fell 1% and organic revenue stayed flat. PepsiCo then lowered its 2026 profit growth forecast.

### How much extra volume does a price cut need?

To keep the same gross profit, divide the discount by (gross margin minus discount). With a 40% margin, a 10% cut needs 33% more volume. A 15% cut needs 60% more.

### What should sales teams do instead of discounting?

Find the real objection, trade instead of giving, put end dates on promotions, offer a different package instead of a lower price, and add value the customer can feel.

### Are store brands growing?

Yes. US store brands set a sales record of $282.8 billion in 2025 and reached a record 23.8% unit share in the first half of 2026.
