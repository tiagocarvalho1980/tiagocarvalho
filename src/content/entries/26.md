A discount lives longer than the quarter you signed it in. You pay it in margin, in customer expectation, in how the price looks when you try to raise it again. The contract is not written on paper. It is written in the customer's memory. And that memory is longer than the sales cycle that caused it.

## The method

**1. Separate "too expensive" from "not enough value."**

When a customer pushes back on price, find out which problem it is before you touch the number. One needs a discount. The other needs a different pitch. Most of the time, it is the second one.

**2. Trade, never give.**

If the price must move, extract something: a longer term, a bigger volume, faster payment, a case study, a referral. A free discount teaches the buyer that asking works, and they will ask again. A traded discount teaches the buyer that your price has a reason.

**3. Change the offer, not the price.**

Build a smaller pack, a lighter service tier, a different scope. Give the customer a cheaper option without cheapening the main one. The product line splits in two; the headline price stays whole.

## Today's practice

Pick one deal where a discount was agreed in the last quarter. Calculate how much extra volume you would need to break even on that cut — divide the discount percentage by (gross margin − discount). Put that number in front of the person who approved it. Most discounts die quietly when that math arrives.

## Where this applies

Any team that sells. Hospitality quoting a group rate. SaaS reps closing an enterprise deal. Agencies negotiating a retainer. Hotels pricing the fourth-quarter corporate contract. The temptation is the same everywhere. So is the long contract that follows.
