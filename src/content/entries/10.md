The best openings in a career almost never look like openings. They look like a job below your level, a task nobody else wants, or a role that sounds slightly odd. Most people filter those out. The ones who don't end up with access that a better title would never have given them.

## The method

**1. Investigate what looks strange.**

When a role, an invitation or a request doesn't fit the usual pattern, that's the signal to look closer, not to walk away. Ask what the position actually gives you access to: the rooms, the people, the information. Judge it by the access, not by the title on the door.

**2. When your idea is rejected, attach it to their problem.**

An idea that gets a no usually failed because it was pitched as your idea. Find the problem the other person already wants solved, and re-present the same idea as the answer to that. The idea doesn't change. The reason to say yes does. Most rejected ideas are one reframe away from approval.

**3. Turn access into evidence, and honour who gave it.**

Access on its own is worth nothing. Use it to produce one measurable result before you ask anyone to believe in the bigger plan. Then protect the relationship with whoever opened the door. Talent gets you in. Character decides whether you're invited back.

## Today's practice

Pick one idea someone said no to in the last six months. Write down the problem that person is currently trying to solve. Rewrite your pitch so the idea is the answer to that problem, and send it today.

## Where this applies

Early-career sales roles, anyone starting again in a new company or country, founders pitching a partner, and hospitality teams where the front desk sees more than the office does. If you've been told "that's not your job", this rule is for you.
